What is Trending in Korea Right Now? A Seoul Salaryman's 2026 Take

S
Korean Salaryman
August 2, 2026
Read time 28 min
SUMMARY

As of August 2026, Korea is seeing a significant shift towards individual autonomy and well-being, evidenced by the rise of N-jobbers and the 'healthy pleasure' movement. Beyond mass media, Koreans are curating hyper-personalized experiences and embracing an analog revival to find balance in their hyper-digital lives.

📅 Updated 2026.08.02
Key takeaways
  • As of August 2026, Koreans are redefining work with 'N-jobber' side hustles and prioritizing 'healthy pleasure' in their diets and lifestyles.
  • The focus is shifting from mass culture to hyper-personalized experiences, allowing individuals to curate their own unique K-culture.
  • Countering digital fatigue, an 'analog revival' sees many embracing film cameras, vinyl, and offline spaces for a digital detox.
👤 This is for readers who want an authentic, on-the-ground understanding of what's genuinely trending in Korea right now, beyond the typical tourist guides or media headlines.

You've landed here because you're curious about what is trending in Korea right now, especially as we hit August 02, 2026. The world watches K-Pop and K-Dramas, but living here, I can tell you the real currents run deeper than what hits your Netflix queue. Things move fast in Seoul, always have, thanks to our palli-palli (the hurry-hurry, do-it-now mindset). So, let's talk about what's actually happening on the ground, in my office, and in the cafes I visit.

The Rise of the N-Jobber (N잡러): More Than Just a Side Hustle

Forget the traditional career ladder; right now, one of the biggest shifts I see around me is the explosion of the N-jobber (N잡러) culture. An N-jobber is someone who holds multiple jobs or side hustles, essentially having 'N' number of professions. It’s not just about making extra cash anymore; it’s about diversifying income streams, finding personal fulfillment outside the main job, and a quiet rebellion against the old 'work till you drop' mentality. Many foreigners assume Koreans are only focused on corporate success, but that's not the whole story anymore. My younger coworkers, especially, are constantly talking about their side gigs – running online stores, teaching languages after hours, even developing indie games. In my own team, one junior colleague designs custom phone cases, and another manages a small vintage clothing shop on Instagram. The office doesn’t discourage it, as long as it doesn’t interfere with our main duties, and frankly, many managers are probably doing something similar themselves. It's a way to feel more secure in an uncertain economy and to carve out a unique identity beyond the company name card. The typical 9-to-6 (often 9-to-8, let's be real) is still demanding, but these extra pursuits provide a necessary escape and a sense of ownership over one's time and skills.

Point: The N-jobber trend is less about greed and more about agency and resilience in modern Korean life.

Healthy Pleasure (헬시플레저): Making Wellness Actually Enjoyable

Another huge wave that's really changing what is trending in Korea right now is 'healthy pleasure' (헬시플레저). This isn't just about dieting or grueling workouts; it’s about making health and wellness an enjoyable, sustainable part of everyday life. Gone are the days of tasteless diet foods and extreme deprivation. Instead, people are seeking out delicious, healthy alternatives and engaging in fitness activities that genuinely bring them joy. Think protein-packed ice cream that actually tastes good, zero-sugar sodas that are genuinely refreshing, or plant-based convenience store meals that are surprisingly gourmet. At the convenience store near my office, the selection of 'healthy pleasure' snacks has exploded in the last year – from high-fiber jelly to low-calorie protein drinks. Even hoesik (the after-work company dinner) menus are starting to offer healthier options, though the soju (Korean distilled spirit) still flows freely, of course. For exercise, it's less about pounding the treadmill and more about Pilates, bouldering, or even 'walking challenges' that integrate nature. My company recently started a lunchtime walking club, and it's surprisingly popular – a small step towards wellness that feels more like a break than a chore. Foreigners often think Korean food is all about BBQ and fried chicken, but there's a huge, delicious world of healthy eating emerging too.

Point: Healthy pleasure is proof that Koreans want to live well, not just look good, and they’re finding tasty ways to do it.

Hyper-Personalized Experiences: Crafting Your Own K-Culture

While K-Pop and K-Dramas still dominate global headlines, on the ground, what is trending in Korea right now is a pivot towards hyper-personalized experiences. The days of simply consuming mass-produced K-culture are giving way to a desire for bespoke, unique, and highly individual forms of expression. People aren't just following big trends; they're curating their own 'My Own K-Culture.' This manifests in countless ways: from custom-made phone cases with unique designs and personalized perfume blending workshops, to 'pop-up' stores dedicated to incredibly niche fandoms or local artists. You see it in the explosion of independent cafes, each with its own distinct vibe and specialty, moving far beyond the major chains. My younger sister recently spent an entire afternoon at a studio where you design your own rings – not for an engagement, just for fun. She posted it all over her social media. Even in dining, it's not just about famous restaurants; it's about finding hidden gems, unique omakase (chef's choice) experiences, or restaurants that cater to specific dietary preferences. Tourists might flock to Myeongdong for branded goods, but locals are hunting for unique finds in Seongsu-dong or Ikseon-dong, seeking out that one-of-a-kind item or experience that nobody else has. It's about personal storytelling through consumption.

Point: K-culture is evolving into millions of individual stories, not just a few blockbusters.

The Analog Revival: Disconnecting to Reconnect

In a country as digitally advanced as Korea, perhaps it's no surprise that what is trending in Korea right now is a strong counter-movement: the analog revival. After years of hyper-connectivity, always-on smartphones, and endless scrolling, many Koreans are actively seeking out ways to disconnect and embrace the tangible. This isn't just about nostalgia; it’s a conscious effort to find peace and focus. Film cameras are incredibly popular again, especially among younger people who've only known digital photography. Vinyl record shops are thriving, offering a slow, deliberate way to experience music. There are cafes designed specifically as 'digital detox' zones where Wi-Fi is intentionally weak, or where you're encouraged to put your phone away and read a physical book. I recently spent a weekend at a traditional hanok (Korean traditional house) guesthouse where the only entertainment was a stack of old books and a quiet courtyard. It was surprisingly refreshing. Even in my daily commute on the Seoul subway, I’m seeing more people reading physical newspapers or books rather than just staring at their phones. This trend challenges the perception that Koreans are always rushing forward with technology; sometimes, we're taking a deliberate step back to appreciate simpler, more tactile experiences.

Point: The analog revival is a quiet but powerful rebellion against digital overload, offering a much-needed break from the always-on world.

The things that trip people up

Q. What is an N-jobber in Korea?

A. An N-jobber (N잡러) is a Korean individual who holds multiple jobs or side hustles, often balancing a primary corporate job with additional creative or entrepreneurial pursuits for financial stability and personal fulfillment.

Q. What does 'healthy pleasure' mean in the context of Korean trends?

A. Healthy pleasure (헬시플레저) refers to the trend of pursuing health and wellness through enjoyable and sustainable methods, moving away from strict diets or grueling workouts towards delicious healthy foods and fun fitness activities.

Q. How are Koreans seeking personalized experiences these days?

A. Koreans are increasingly curating 'My Own K-Culture' by seeking out bespoke items, participating in custom workshops (like perfume blending or ring design), visiting unique independent cafes, and finding niche pop-up stores that reflect their individual tastes, rather than just consuming mainstream trends.

Q. Is the analog revival popular in technologically advanced Korea?

A. Yes, the analog revival is a growing counter-trend where many Koreans, especially younger generations, are embracing non-digital experiences like using film cameras, listening to vinyl records, and visiting 'digital detox' cafes to find respite from constant digital connectivity.

Q. Are these trends mainly for younger Koreans?

A. While younger generations often spearhead these trends, the desire for better work-life balance, enjoyable wellness, personalized expression, and digital breaks is increasingly resonating across various age groups in Korea, including many in their 30s and 40s.
